# Wuki: How To Know If You're Being Authentic
**Length / Source:** Episode 11 | Tue, 29 Oct 2024 | 54m | source: whisper
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Wuki argues that authenticity — chasing the feeling of making something you genuinely love rather than chasing streams, trends, or comparison to peers — is both the real source of career longevity and, paradoxically, the most reliable path to commercial connection, since audiences can subconsciously sense when work is chased versus genuine.

## Key claims & mental models
- Success is reframed away from external metrics ("I'm not chasing success, I'm not chasing numbers") toward chasing a specific internal feeling — the satisfaction of finishing something you personally love — because that feeling is renewable indefinitely, while streams/attention are not.
- Coming from outside a genre (his own path: pop-punk/rock band → EDM/bass/house) is framed as an advantage, not a detour — cross-genre taste produces more distinctive work than being raised purely inside one scene, where new output just becomes "a new iteration" of what already exists.
- Deep, wide-ranging musical knowledge (cites Bjork, George Benson, the Prodigy, Chemical Brothers) is treated as a functional asset, not trivia — foundational knowledge of a genre's history is framed as part of what lets some producers consistently make things that "do well today."
- Explicitly rejects the "tortured artist" myth — he states he cannot write well from a bad headspace; feeding his life (hiking, travel, relationships, occasional psychedelics) is a precondition for good work, not a distraction from it.
- Trend-awareness and authenticity aren't opposed — he describes consciously noticing genre "waves" coming (e.g., a house-music resurgence) but choosing to commit to what he already loved rather than chase the wave opportunistically, and frames that self-honesty as what let him "find himself as an artist."
- Comparison/jealousy toward peers ("feeding the bad wolf") is named as one of the most corrosive forces against creativity — it leads to trend-chasing and self-questioning, which he calls "the worst recipe for being creative."
- Never having one explosive viral "blow up" moment is reframed as lucky rather than unlucky — sudden peaks are hard to return to and can trap an artist into chasing a repeat of that one moment instead of building steadily.
- A healthy peer network functions as a non-transactional feedback loop (trusted friends who get early/rough ideas) that's explicitly separated from the more guarded, industry-facing relationships (bigger DJs, labels) who only see more finished work.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Keep a small, trusted circle of peers you send early/unfinished ideas to purely for honest feedback and mutual encouragement — deliberately keep this separate from what you send to bigger labels/DJs, which should be far more polished.
- Use a non-musician, zero-context listener (a partner, a friend, even a kid) as a gut-check: play them a new track cold and watch whether they react instantly, without needing the track explained to them — an unprompted, unexplainable "I like it" is treated as more diagnostic than technical self-justification.
- When you notice a genre "wave" building, explicitly ask what you actually love right now, independent of the wave, before deciding whether/how to move toward it.
- Study why something with mass appeal is connecting, even if it's not your taste — treat dismissing a big song as "not deserving" of its success as a losing mindset, since the audience decides, not the artist.
- Note his own growth example: viral traction came from low-production, filmed-at-the-desk mashup/edit videos (a Busta Rhymes flip, a Vengaboys flip, an Enya/Lil Jon "Crunk in Time" flip that hit roughly 7 million views) — high concept, not high budget, drove the breakthrough.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Before you finish and release your next track, play it once for someone with zero technical vocabulary and no context — a partner, a non-musician friend, even a kid — and watch their first, unprompted reaction instead of explaining the track to them. Wuki describes doing exactly this with his now-wife: the moment he started explaining the reference or the clever chord choice, he'd lost the read, because she either likes it or she doesn't — you can't explain someone into liking a song. Most artists' feedback loops run through other producers, who react to technique; this is a fast, free way to test whether a track is "undeniable" on a purely emotional level, which is closer to how most of the actual audience will experience it.
