# Wenzday: How To Build A Strong Relationship With Your Audience
**Length / Source:** Episode 13 | Tue, 12 Nov 2024 | 64m | source: whisper
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Wenzday argues that a durable artist-audience relationship comes from building a legible, character-driven "universe" around your music (persona, visual world, narrative arc) rather than releasing songs in isolation, and from treating both your live crowd and your inner circle as real relationships worth investing in rather than pure content/growth inputs.

## Key claims & mental models
- An early run-in with typecasting in musical theater/child acting (being cast in racially stereotyped roles despite training for lead parts) is presented as the origin of her willingness to pivot rather than wait for an industry to change around her.
- DJ AM and later Steve Aoki were used as literal business-model proof points — she pitched her parents on DJing as a viable career by pointing to AM as the person who first made DJ residencies (and DJing generally) a legitimate, rock-star-tier career path, then modeled her own multi-hyphenate ambitions (music into fashion/pop culture) on Aoki specifically because "here's a person who looks like me."
- Realized that being "one-dimensional" (just an open-format DJ) has a ceiling (cites ageism/glass ceilings in that circuit), which is what pushed her into production and, eventually, into building a full artist persona rather than just a skill set.
- The "Wenzday" persona is explicitly built as a "hyperbolized version" of herself — a character that can do/say things she's personally too shy or scared to (traces back to crippling stage fright; a manager told her to study pro-wrestling crowd work, which led her to build a confident stage character as protection).
- Picking a globally-recognizable reference point for a project name/persona (Wednesday Addams: "badass female," dark/punk/feminist coded) is described as deliberate — it lets even someone who doesn't speak your language infer your vibe instantly from the name and iconography alone, without needing translation.
- Her breakout project, "Heartbreak House," was pitched and built not as four songs but as a full conceptual world (a 4-track EP where each track maps to a stage of a breakup, with an accompanying narrative/visual universe) — this got her rejected by multiple labels first (one team internally called it her "manifesto," not kindly) before Insomniac signed it specifically because it was a world they could build on, not just tracks.
- Industry rejection is reframed through Cher's "Believe" story (dropped by management/label multiple times before her biggest hit) — the lesson drawn is that gatekeeper "no"s are not predictive, and if something feels true in your gut, ride it till the wheels fall off.
- The business side of music is structurally pattern-seeking and pessimistic ("doesn't fit our formula") in a way that's genuinely taxing over time — the explicit counterweight is a small, trusted circle of peers who give specific, real creative feedback (this line opens and closes the episode, functioning as its thesis).
- Deliberately talks to her live crowd on the mic to give context for riskier or less-familiar song choices (e.g., introducing an obscure trance record before dropping it) — treats the in-room audience as the actual point, explicitly contrasting this with peers who are visibly playing to the after-movie/TikTok clip instead of the room.
- Draws an explicit boundary between her personal life (relationship/dating) and her artist-facing social accounts, framing this as a real, under-discussed sacrifice artists make, not secrecy.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- When building a project or persona, choose a name/reference point that's globally legible at a glance (a widely-known character or archetype) so your visual identity communicates instantly, even across language barriers.
- Package even a small release (as few as 4 songs) as a conceptual "universe" — a through-line narrative, a visual world, a rollout story — rather than presenting isolated tracks, when pitching labels or your own audience.
- If stage fright or self-consciousness is blocking performance, build a deliberate character/persona (study crowd-work from an unrelated performance discipline, e.g. pro wrestling) as a structured way to access confidence rather than trying to force it as "yourself."
- On the mic during a set, briefly narrate context for unfamiliar or deep-cut song choices instead of assuming the crowd already gets it — treat that framing as part of the performance.
- Study your genre's actual history (she cites reading a foundational dance-music history book cover-to-cover) as a way of earning legitimacy and craft respect, not just marketing tactics.
- Keep a small, real feedback circle (peers/friends in your scene) who'll give specific, constructive input (key/BPM-level notes), and treat maintaining that circle as directly protective of both mental health and output quality.
- When you're tired or on "autopilot" during a long touring stretch, deliberately choose the harder/riskier set over the safe, expected one on at least some nights, to keep the craft itself alive.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Before your next release — even if it's only 2-4 songs — decide what "world" it belongs to: a visual identity, a one-line narrative arc, a character or point of view that ties the songs together, the way Wenzday framed "Heartbreak House" as four stages of a breakup inside a named universe rather than four unrelated tracks. That framing is what got a major dance label to say yes after several passes, but the underlying move is available at any scale: most independent artists drop songs one at a time with no connective tissue, so simply telling your audience (and yourself) what world this release lives in is a free, immediate way to make a small release read as a body of work instead of a one-off.
