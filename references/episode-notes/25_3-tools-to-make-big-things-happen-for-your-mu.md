# How to Find The RIGHT People to Help You Build Your Music Project (For Music Artists)
**Length / Source:** Episode 25 | Thu, 13 Feb 2025 | 13m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** The solo artist model is obsolete for building a real career — modern artists need to assemble a small, startup-style "band" of five specialized roles (producer, creative director, marketer, executive manager, performer) instead of trying to master everything alone.

## Key claims & mental models
- Traditional bands (guitar/bass/drums/vocals) existed out of technical necessity — you needed four bodies to physically make the sound; modern production tools and AI removed that constraint, so the reason to build a team today is skills, not instrumentation.
- The "one-man/one-woman band" is framed as functionally dead for anyone trying to scale — not because solo music-making doesn't work, but because building an audience now requires specialized skills (marketing, business, brand) that rarely live in one person.
- Five essential modern-band roles: producer (sound architect), creative director (builds the story, characters, and visual universe), marketer (launch strategy, content, PR — a role most artists dislike but "marketeers" genuinely love), executive manager (contracts, budgets, deal-closing, relationship-building), and performer (the charismatic face/voice, which can be a different person than the producer or creative director).
- The executive manager role is systematically undervalued/disrespected by artists because it feels less "creative," but the author argues it's often the actual difference between a mid-level and top-tier artist.
- Claimed advantages of the team model over solo or traditional-band structures: less drama/fewer egos, faster decisions (no need for the whole group in the room), higher output quality (specialists doing what they're best at), and the business side is built in from day one instead of bolted on later.
- Real groups are cited as evidence this structure already works informally: Brockhampton (distinct creative director, producer, singer, visuals, and manager roles), Odd Future (Tyler as creative director, Frank Ocean on songwriting, Earl on lyrics, Taco/Jasper on social and comic relief), Billie Eilish/Finneas (performer + producer duo), The Chainsmokers (producer + formally-trained marketer), Gorillaz (visual artist/creative director + musician), Tame Impala (producer/songwriter + manager-strategist).
- Some artists cover two roles themselves (Tyler the Creator: creative director + performer; Fred again: performer + producer + marketing instincts) but still bring in outside collaborators, especially management, to fill remaining gaps.
- Team-building principle: recruit based on who already loves and is good at a function, not on formal titles or industry credentials — "find people who love the vision and then play to strengths, not titles."

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Identify which single role (producer, creative director, marketer, manager, performer) you naturally are, then deliberately go find people for the other four rather than trying to cover them all yourself.
- Look inside your existing circle first — a friend obsessed with social platforms is a marketing-lead candidate; a friend skilled on production software is a producer candidate — before searching externally.
- If a marketing-minded collaborator isn't found organically, post an explicit listing (the author's own example: posting to a job board like Indeed with something like "music group seeks marketing director").
- The manager role doesn't require someone with prior industry experience — the author's own manager was a close friend with zero management background who grew into the role alongside him.
- Use the named real-world groups (Brockhampton, Odd Future, Chainsmokers, etc.) as literal templates: map who in your own network could plausibly fill each of the five roles.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Most independent artists quietly stall out because they're trying to be the producer, creative director, marketer, and manager simultaneously — and marketing/business development is a genuinely different skill set (often a different personality type) from making music. This week, name a specific person — even an inexperienced friend — for each of the other four roles, starting with marketing, the role artists resist most but that determines whether anyone ever hears the music. Recruiting someone who "loves marketing the way you love making art" is more available, and higher-leverage, than most artists assume.
