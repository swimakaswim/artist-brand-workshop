# How To Build An Audience Of Millions Of Fans
**Length / Source:** Episode 7 | Tue, 01 Oct 2024 | 9m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Fans follow artists for one or more of seven specific, evolutionarily-rooted psychological reasons (attraction, aspiration, sameness, entertainment, status, novelty, guidance), and once an artist identifies which reason(s) drive their actual audience, they can deliberately shape content and community strategy to deepen and sustain that specific relationship.

## Key claims & mental models
- People follow stories, not just music — a hardwired survival mechanism tied to Maslow's hierarchy (physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization), inherited from early humans sharing survival- and mate-relevant stories.
- Audiences process a story on two simultaneous levels — the literal/informational content and a subconscious personal-relevance read (e.g., a "bravery" story signals both a threat to know about and the teller's value as a protector/partner).
- Seven reasons people follow an artist: (1) sexual/romantic attraction, (2) aspiration — "I want my life to be like this," (3) sameness/identity mirroring — "we are the same," (4) raw entertainment, (5) status/coolness by association, (6) novelty/intrigue, (7) guidance — the "wise friend"/mentor figure; fans often follow for more than one reason at once, always layered on top of liking the actual art.
- Each reason implies an unspoken contract between artist and fan; content that violates that implicit contract causes quiet disengagement ("the brain will allow for a few strikes, but too many and they're over it").
- Some follow-reasons don't naturally convert into deeper monetizable fandom (e.g., a "wise friend"/tutorial audience may not become ticket buyers) unless the artist deliberately shifts the relationship over time.
- Artists are framed as "community leaders" as much as creators — the audience relationship is symmetrical and reciprocal like any real relationship, and artists are constantly "coaching" fans (intentionally or by default) on what to expect from them.
- Content strategy should flow from which reason(s) dominate a given fan relationship — e.g., a "Jester" archetype should skew ~95% high-energy comedic content; a "magician" archetype (Prince cited) leans into weirdness/intrigue; a "lover" archetype leans into flirtation/sensuality.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Explicitly write down which of the 7 reasons you believe are driving people to follow you — don't default to assuming it's purely "they like my music."
- Run future content and creative ideas through that filter: does this reinforce the specific implicit promise this audience segment is following you for?
- Reference models cited: Ralph Lauren/Polo for aspiration-and-lifestyle branding; Kendrick Lamar for "we are the same" identity-based community building.
- Try the relationship-audit exercise Chris ran with his wife: separately list what you believe your audience values most about following you, and what they're hoping to get — then check whether your current content actually delivers it.
- If your current fan relationship rests on a reason with a natural shelf life (attraction, or "I want to be you" aspiration), deliberately work to add a second, more durable reason (e.g., identity/sameness) before that first driver fades.
- Example content-mix given explicitly: a "Jester"/entertainer brand should run roughly 95% high-level comedic, highly shareable content, reserving heartfelt/real moments for the remainder.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Pick your last 2-3 pieces of content and ask which of the 7 follow-reasons each one was actually serving, then compare that against which reason you believe is driving most of your current audience. A mismatch there — not a lack of content quality — is the likely explanation for flat growth or fans who watch/like but never convert to deeper engagement (buying a ticket, joining a community), because you're breaking the specific implicit promise that got them to follow in the first place.
