# Party Favor: How To STAND OUT In A Crowded Space
**Length / Source:** Thu, 12 Dec 2024 | 72m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Party Favor's career-long interview argues that standing out and lasting in a crowded scene comes less from tactics and more from making art for yourself first, taking creative risks despite fear/imposter syndrome, and actively managing your internal relationship with the critical "voice in your head."

## Key claims & mental models
- Imposter syndrome persisted even after major success ("I don't know if I'll ever feel like I'm good enough") but is reframed as partly useful — it keeps you grounded, working, and striving rather than complacent.
- Fear of regret outweighs fear of failure as a motivator for taking a big creative leap (dropping out of college 16 units short of his degree to chase DJing) — better to try and know you tried than to always wonder "what if."
- His breakout song was made purely for himself with zero audience or career expectations ("I wasn't thinking about how this would sound on a main stage") — that absence of outside pressure is framed as the actual source of its magic, and he describes this as a near-universal origin story across artists/genres.
- After a hit, the "why" behind making music can quietly shift from "I want to make something cool" to "I need another hit or this stops" — and that shift in motivation changes the work and is something audiences can feel.
- Being commercially boxed into the genre that built your career (his case: "twerk") creates real tension between honoring the fans/sound that got you there and the artist's need to keep evolving; he contrasts himself with an artist like Flume, who committed to one identifiable sound for years and built a deeper, more singular fan connection as a result.
- Taste is built cumulatively and unconsciously from everything you're exposed to (family's music, blogs, travel, museums) — deliberately "downloading" references (via travel, art, unrelated media) is treated as active creative input, not a detour from the work.
- Key reframe: separate "who you are" from "the voice in your head" — treat the inner critical voice like a person you care about (ask it what it's afraid of) rather than an enemy to suppress, since suppression just makes it "rebel" later.
- Years of critique-heavy studio work (endlessly assessing loops/mixes) trains a default critical lens that bleeds into how you see people and the world generally — this is named as a specific occupational hazard worth actively counteracting.
- Career breaks involve real luck/timing (a manager friend's promotion-industry connections opened doors he couldn't have cold-called into) — not purely merit or hustle.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- When you catch yourself being reflexively critical (of a stranger, your own work, anything), deliberately generate 3-5 things you genuinely like about it before moving on — a practiced counter to the "default critical setting" creative work can instill.
- Talk to your own inner critical voice like you would a struggling friend — ask directly "what's going on, are you scared?" instead of shutting it down.
- Explicitly praise yourself out loud for things done well (his example: telling himself he'd "killed it" after a solo-parenting weekend) rather than only cataloguing what needs improvement.
- Swap "I have to" for "I get to" in daily language around your work/obligations — described as a recent, genuine mindset shift for him (credited to something his wife heard on a podcast).
- Don't skip the unglamorous build-up: he played small local/house-party gigs and low-paying club sets before any breakout, and credits that slow climb.
- It's fine to learn production with zero formal training — he and a college friend taught themselves in Logic with no music-theory background and produced work he calls "awful" before finding a workable style.

## Highest-leverage insight
> The most repeatable, non-obvious habit here is the "3-to-5 things I like about this" exercise: the moment you notice yourself being reflexively critical — of a stranger, a piece of art, your own last release — stop and consciously name three to five specific things you like about it instead. He frames this as deliberately retraining a default-critical disposition that years of studio/mixing work can quietly install, and pairs it with swapping "I have to" for "I get to" in how you talk to yourself about the work. Both cost nothing, take seconds, and directly target the inner-critic spiral that (per this episode) is what actually derails artists under pressure — not lack of talent or opportunity.
