# How To Find Your Signature Sound - And Why Some Artists Never Do
**Length / Source:** Episode 28 | Wed, 30 Apr 2025 | 24m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Top-tier artists deliberately define a consistent set of sonic "ingredients" and commit to them for a season, rather than letting streaming numbers dictate what to make next, and that intentional consistency — not raw talent — is what separates six-figure artists from mid-tier ones.

## Key claims & mental models
- A defined sound is the "soundtrack" half of the brand-as-movie metaphor used across the channel — story and sound have to be paired deliberately, the way a film pairs script and score.
- The core trap: artists make "everything under the sun," see which song performs best, then chase more of that — which the author calls "letting the audience tell you what to make instead of defining what you want to make."
- Historical framing: 50+ years ago a fixed band lineup and limited gear naturally constrained a group's palette, so a consistent sound emerged by default; modern production tools and AI removed that constraint, making "what to make" harder, not easier, because everything is now possible.
- Central operating metaphor is cooking: lay out your specific "ingredients" (drums, synths, vocal style, key, subject matter, etc.) on a mental counter — you can make many different dishes (genres, tempos, moods) from them, but they'll all still taste like you (cited example: Skrillex making pop, dubstep, hip-hop, and UKG that all still sound like Skrillex).
- A clear sound reduces decision friction for everyone else too — festivals, collaborators, and brands instantly know what they're getting and how you fit.
- Originality is framed as recombination, not invention from nothing — illustrated with a taco-truck anecdote (a half-Korean, half-Hispanic owner combining Korean BBQ with Mexican burritos outsold every truck on the block) as a model for building an authentic personal "recipe" from genuine influences rather than copying a single existing act.
- Fans use sound (paired with story) to define their own identity, similar to wearing a band t-shirt; being recognized as the originator of a sound matters because copycats reinforce the original artist's position in the audience's mind ("this sounds like so-and-so").
- Commitment to a body of work (measured in time or song count), not one-off singles, is the key behavioral difference between top-tier and mid-tier artists — most artists don't break through until they've cycled through the full define → commit → execute → reassess process five or six times.
- It's acceptable to occasionally make something outside the defined sandbox — but the discipline is to finish it, then judge afterward whether it belongs in this project/era or should be shelved for a different one.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Step 1: List all-time favorite songs/albums, the music your parents/household played, and your most meaningful music memories.
- Step 2: List current favorite songs/albums/artists and who is currently pushing you creatively.
- Step 3: "Mix and match" — combine items from the past list with items from the current list into hypothetical hybrid descriptions (author's own worked example: "mid-tempo bass meets 80s synthwave soundtrack meets 80s new wave").
- Step 4: Build an explicit sound chart across fixed categories and write your default choice for each: drum style, synth style, vocal style, key/mode (and the emotional target — sad, triumphant, cool, etc.), subject matter, sample usage, instrumentation, sound design, arrangement, plus a catch-all "other" category.
- Step 5: Commit to a body of work for a defined stretch — either time-boxed (e.g. 12–24 months) or output-boxed (e.g. 40–50 demos, even if only 10–20% get released).
- Step 6: Make the art inside those constraints; if pulled to make something outside the sandbox, finish it, then decide afterward whether it fits this project or should be saved for later.
- Step 7: Once the commitment period ends, reassess and evolve into a new chapter — repeat the cycle.
- Worked case studies applying the same chart to real artists (Calvin Harris's 2011–2012 run, Purple Disco Machine, Kanye West across eras, LSDXOXO/LSD Dream) to show a consistent underlying "recipe" beneath surface-level genre variety.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Instead of waiting for streaming data to tell you what your sound is, sit down for one session and fill out the literal chart this episode describes — drums, synths, vocals, key/mode, subject matter, samples, instrumentation, sound design, arrangement — writing your current default answer for each row. That single document becomes an instant decision filter for every future session ("does this sample/synth/arrangement fit my chart?") and is the concrete mechanism the episode credits for the gap between artists who release scattered, forgettable one-offs and artists who build a body of work that compounds into a recognizable identity.
