# How to Build a Music Brand (Full Masterclass)
**Length / Source:** Episode 24 | Tue, 04 Feb 2025 | 28m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Treat your artist project as a movie universe you deliberately design — vision, rules, plot, characters, visuals, execution, and long-term commitment — rather than something that emerges accidentally from just making music.

## Key claims & mental models
- Modern brand = "the movie or show the audience is watching," and it's equally as important as the music itself; the two must work together to hold attention in an oversaturated market.
- Worldbuilding works because humans are biologically wired to attend to stories and music (survival mechanism) — a defined universe is a deliberate lever on that wiring, not just decoration.
- Two universe types to choose between: "play the brand" (fictional/conceptual, e.g. Gorillaz, MF Doom — all about invented characters and setting) vs. "play the person" (reality-show style, e.g. Taylor Swift, Steve Aoki — the brand is the artist's real life).
- Universe "rules" (what's possible/impossible in this world) must be defined before plot or characters — this is screenwriting logic (Star Wars' Force/hyperspace as the reference example), and the more thorough the rules and backstory work upfront, the faster and more consistent every future creative decision becomes.
- Story plot should map to a recognizable narrative shape (references Christopher Booker's seven basic plots: overcoming the monster, rags to riches, the quest, voyage and return, comedy, tragedy, rebirth) — the chosen plot should then inform content strategy, press angles, styling, and copy, not just be background flavor.
- Characters need one primary archetype (max two, and if two, an 80/20 or 90/10 split so it stays legible) plus a matching narrative element that proves it — a hero needs a villain, a rebel needs something to rebel against, a lover needs someone/something to love.
- Clarity — not talent — is what separates artists with small, confused audiences from artists with soldout international tours; the author names this as the single biggest recurring gap he sees when auditing client brands.
- Notably, the author admits he could execute this clearly for clients but struggled to do it for his own artist project — his business partner diagnosed it as a point-of-view problem: he could be objective (coach-like) about others' projects but brought personal baggage and perfectionism to his own.
- Vision without execution fails just like a great script with the wrong production team — hiring genuinely skilled collaborators is treated as equally important as the creative vision itself.
- Consistency beats novelty-chasing: commit to a defined universe for at least 12–24 months before pivoting, since abandoning a concept after a few weeks or months means never finding out what it could have become.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Step 1: Journal on *why* you're building this — creative outlet, business, community, or legacy — because the dominant motive changes what tradeoffs (e.g. creative control vs. paid collaboration) you'll accept later.
- Step 2: Decide explicitly whether the brand is about a fictional universe/characters or about you as the real person.
- Step 3: Write out the universe's rules and backstories before writing any story or content, the way a screenwriter (cites Tarantino) preps deep backstory so any on-set question has a fast, consistent answer.
- Step 4: Pick your plot type (from the seven basic plots) and let it drive interviews, styling, and social copy.
- Step 5: Define your primary character archetype (max two, 80/20 split) and the specific story element that makes it legible to an audience (the obstacle/foe/love object it needs).
- Step 6: Write a one-sentence elevator pitch and a short narrative-style bio using the plot/archetype filters (worked examples given for Taylor Swift, Eminem, Fred again).
- Step 7: Build visual mood boards — reference images for photography/illustration style, motion, color, fonts, styling; keep one strong image per category rather than overloading. Worked case study: a Chris Lake × Chris Lorenzo duo project built around "vintage UK rave meets contact sheets," a recurring yellow sticker motif, and a crown icon nodding to their UK roots.
- Step 8: Execute by hiring the best available collaborators for each craft — treat this like a Hollywood director building a trusted regular crew.
- Step 9: Compile everything into a brand guidelines deck (bio, personality/voice/tone, mood boards, logo usage rules, colors, fonts, press photos) to send to contractors, promoters, and labels — the author notes that, as a former 15-year talent buyer, receiving a real brand deck immediately signaled a more serious (and more bookable) artist to him.
- Step 10: Build a content calendar/release schedule and treat every touchpoint (posts, bio, videos, interviews, EP interludes) as a scene in the larger story.
- Bonus: commit to the built universe for 12–24 months minimum before making major changes.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Do the archetype-plus-obstacle exercise from Step 5 this week: pick exactly one character archetype for yourself (or a second only at an 80/20 split), then write down, in one line, the specific thing your archetype needs to be legible — what you're fighting, rebelling against, or devoted to. Most artists skip straight to "what should I post" without ever naming this, which is why their content feels disconnected. Once that one line exists, use it to write the fill-in-the-blank elevator pitch ("a [archetype] artist who [does X], up against [obstacle]") — that sentence becomes the filter for every bio, caption, and visual choice going forward instead of each one being improvised from scratch.
