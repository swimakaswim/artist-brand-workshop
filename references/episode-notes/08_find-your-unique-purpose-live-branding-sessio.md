# Find Your Unique Purpose, Live Branding Session with Tor
**Length / Source:** Episode 8 | Tue, 08 Oct 2024 | 53m | source: whisper
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Chris Alba coaches producer Tor Ayman through the idea that even a foundational branding decision like whether to use a stage name has to be derived from a clear, singular message the artist wants to send — not chosen first as a cosmetic decision — and that for Tor specifically, that message is built on radical transparency about the real, unglamorous creative process.

## Key claims & mental models
- "You're talking to me about the title of the movie when we don't really know what the movie is yet" — symbol-level decisions (a name, a logo) shouldn't be made before the underlying story/message is defined; get the "movie" clear first.
- Two-audience model: every artist brand actually serves two different audiences simultaneously — the public/casual audience (interested in your life and lifestyle) and the industry audience (labels, A&Rs, other artists) — and a brand decision has to work for both.
- Reframing "getting lucky": Tor's break (a friend overhearing a producer looking for an intern) looked like pure luck, but he'd effectively logged tens of thousands of hours of prior preparation — the opportunity only converted into a career because he was already ready for it.
- A believable, differentiated brand message identified for Tor: most successful producers only flex the lifestyle result of success; almost none actively champion the message that ordinary, relatable people can do extraordinary things too — that gap is presented as Tor's available position, with Pharrell's multi-medium, point-of-view-driven public persona cited as an adjacent model.
- Brand personality archetypes reused as a framework (12 types — hero, outlaw, explorer, everyman, lover, jester, ruler, etc.); Diplo is given as a worked "explorer" example — his brand is built entirely around exploring places, sounds, and himself, and fans who see themselves as explorers bond to that.
- The alias-vs-real-name question resolves itself once the message is set: if the brand's core ethos is realness and pulling back the curtain, a stage alias reads as "a made-up character" that undercuts that message — so keeping his real name is the consistent choice.
- Emotional accessibility matters more than polish for bonding: showing that "everybody's just a guy or a woman" doing the work makes success feel attainable to the audience rather than distant or manufactured.
- Once an artist has real clarity on their message, execution speeds up dramatically — content decisions stop requiring deliberation, the same way songwriting gets faster once direction is set.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Before choosing a stage name, logo, or visual identity, write down one sentence answering "what is my movie about, and how do I want to affect the people watching it?" — use that sentence to test every symbolic decision afterward.
- Pull back the curtain deliberately: post the unpolished, in-progress "kitchen session" moments of making music, not only finished/polished studio content — this is presented as a direct differentiator, not just supporting content.
- Modeled tactic: Mike Shinoda's Twitch streams, where he'd record two versions of a part (e.g., a bass line played on bass guitar vs. the same part on a synth) and ask the live audience which they preferred — reported splits were consistently around 80/20 or 90/10. Use small, real-time creative choices like this as a lightweight way to involve the audience, without fully crowd-sourcing the art (explicitly not recommended as a general practice).
- Songwriting-craft note mentioned in passing: aim for melody-first, internationally singable hooks — "melody is king" — over lyric-first writing, if broad connection is the goal.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Before touching your logo, your name, or your visual identity, write one sentence defining what you want your brand's "movie" to be about and how you want to affect the people watching it — then test every symbolic decision against that sentence instead of choosing symbols first. For a working producer specifically, the differentiator most people skip is showing the real, unglamorous parts of making the work (a livestreamed A/B choice between two bass takes, a "here's what actually happened in the room" caption) instead of only posting the polished result — that transparency is itself the position, not just content to fill the gap between releases.
