# How to Visually Upgrade Your Music Brand: Art Direction Playbook (Mau P Edition)
**Length / Source:** Episode 40 | Sat, 29 Nov 2025 | 41m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** A real rebrand — a new story, position, uniqueness, sound, visual identity, and content plan, not a new logo — is what took the same artist from a few thousand dollars a show to six figures-plus per show, and the full process (using Maurice West's rebrand into Mau P as the case study) is repeatable.

## Key claims & mental models
- A rebrand is not a new logo — it's a new story and position in the culture; the underlying talent/music quality can stay identical while the business outcome changes entirely (Mau P case: same artist, few thousand dollars/show → six figures+/show).
- Five scenarios that signal it's time to rebrand: stuck with no growth; "everything all at once" (identity blurred by years of unfocused experimentation); a reputation mismatch (an old identity, e.g. promoter, undercuts the new one); bad packaging (good music, unclear/incohesive story and visuals); or a genuine mission change (Chris Lake's 2016–17 shift from big-room/progressive house toward a "cooler," Skrillex/Oliver Heldens-adjacent sound and scene).
- Point of view is claimed to be roughly 80% of what makes a brand work; actually sharing that point of view and finding like-minded people is the remaining 20% (Christopher Nolan cited as an artist whose stories vary but whose point of view stays recognizable even with no budget or reputation).
- A rebrand doesn't require burning everything down — keep what's working, change what isn't, but the changes need to be significant enough to register as a genuinely new frame.
- Brand-building framework (the "movie"): define the master/controlling concept first, derive the archetype from it, then define the plot via what the character wants — Mau P is framed as 80% rebel / 20% ruler, plot-mapped to "overcoming the monster," where the monster is the mainstream system, not a person.
- A brand's position/point of view should be written as explicit belief statements ("we believe / we do not believe...") — a brand is treated as a set of beliefs, not just music or a logo.
- Once story and position exist, a layer of specific, textural "uniqueness" details is what keeps an archetype from feeling generic (for Mau P: hometown/underground pride, producing in his late father's home studio, "controlled" vs. raw chaos, calm-offstage/high-energy-onstage contrast, referencing past influences instead of current trends).
- The "superpower" framework: headline-level artists tend to win on one dominant differentiator out of five — content volume (e.g. Steve Aoki, James Hype), music quality (e.g. Zedd, Max Styler), relatability/likability (e.g. Dom Dolla, John Summit), performance/creativity (e.g. Anyma, Eric Prydz), or "cool factor" (e.g. Fisher, Skrillex, Chris Lake) — Mau P's chosen superpower is cool factor.
- Visual identity should be assembled as a formal mood board/brand deck (personality & archetype write-up, a short fixed set of "visual brand pillars," photography style spec, posing references, video style references, texture and type treatment) built before any shoot happens, so it can also brief any hired creative team.
- Content should be planned top-down as a blueprint that matches the story: content buckets first, then a 12-month calendar with tentpole moments, then a 6-month detail pass, then a day-by-day 30-day plan — not posted ad hoc.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Write brand belief statements using "we believe / we do not believe..." sentence stems before doing any visual work — treat this as necessary scaffolding, not fluff.
- Write an "elevator pitch" for your brand like the summary on the back of a DVD case — one tight paragraph establishing character, world, and attitude.
- Structure a mood board/brand deck in this order: title/logo page → personality & archetype write-up → visual brand pillars (a short fixed list of visual "ingredients," e.g. Mau P's: himself, 2000s nostalgia, unpolished/candid photography, luxury streetwear) → overall mood board page → photography style spec → posing reference page → video style reference(s) → texture treatment → font/type usage → one combined example (e.g. a show flyer) showing every element working together at once.
- Comment "cult playbook" on the host's videos for a free art direction playbook/mood-board template — no email or signup required.
- Content bucket examples used for the Mau P relaunch: curated + candid photo shoots; selfie content (bathroom/car/plane shots); show-footage clips limited to standout moments only, not generic recaps; music videos, show announcements, and custom Spotify canvas videos; lead marketing around ONE defining track instead of a flood of edits/bootlegs; and content built to be remixed by fans (memes, edits, track-ID threads).
- Plan content top-down across four horizons: content buckets → 12-month calendar with tentpoles → 6-month detail pass → day-by-day 30-day plan across all platforms.
- Once initial assets exist, compile them into a brand guide/"brand bible" so promoters, labels, and contractors can quickly reference the story, position, and visual rules.
- Before launching a rebrand, write down every reason that might make you want to quit or abandon it — naming the risk in advance defuses it and primes problem-solving before it happens.
- Commit to a new brand identity for a minimum of 24 months before judging whether it worked — story and recognition compound slowly.
- Even "spontaneous" viral moments can be planned in advance: Mau P's "Mau P is dancing" Coachella response to online trolling (about his lack of visible energy onstage) was staged, not improvised.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Do two of the exercises described for the Mau P rebrand this week, without touching a camera or a post: (1) pick ONE of the five "superpowers" — content volume, music quality, relatability, performance/creativity, or cool factor — as the thing you're actually trying to win at, instead of spreading effort evenly across all five; and (2) write 8–10 brand belief statements starting with "we believe" / "we do not believe." Together, one sentence of focus and one page of belief will tell you more about what to post, wear, and say next than any amount of new content would. Most stalled artist brands don't have a content-volume problem — they have an undefined-position problem that more posting can't fix.
