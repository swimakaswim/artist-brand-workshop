# Stop Wasting Your Time- A Time Management Guide For Artists and Creatives
**Length / Source:** Episode 6 | Tue, 24 Sep 2024 | 10m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Artists get more creative time not by finding extra hours but by engineering how they work — knowing their own rhythms, batching similar tasks, time-blocking everything (including rest), and treating both recovery and saying no as active productivity tools rather than failures.

## Key claims & mental models
- "Know thyself" is the foundation: self-knowledge of your working style (pressure vs. steady pace, habitual vs. varied, morning vs. evening, home vs. public spaces) should drive your schedule design — there's no universal correct rhythm.
- High-output creatives maintain a consistent personal "rhythm" (e.g., banking weeks of music then banking weeks of content, or splitting the week itself by task type) rather than working reactively.
- Task-batching protects focus: repeatedly switching between calls, creative work, and errands drains the brain; grouping same-type tasks into contiguous blocks keeps it "in the same sandbox."
- Time-blocking (calendar + task manager) turns intention into a forcing function — blocking down to small personal items (meals, breaks, family time) is presented as what actually makes the important things happen, not overkill.
- "Efficient" (checking tasks off a list) is explicitly distinguished from "effective" (moving the ball toward bigger goals) — busyness isn't the same as progress.
- Time is the ultimate leverage point: decide which tasks genuinely require you and give away the rest ("if you want to go fast, go alone; if you want to go far, go together").
- Saying no is a practiced skill, not a personality trait — protecting blocked time (even time for rest or family) requires being willing to decline requests that feel good to say yes to.
- Recognizing the specific felt sense of oncoming burnout is treated as an actionable skill/cue to proactively recharge (meditation, sauna, cold plunge, exercise, a walk, a movie), rather than something to push through.
- The system's success standard isn't 100% completion — hitting roughly 80% of a planned week is framed as a genuine win, keeping the system sustainable instead of guilt-inducing.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Run a self-audit: do you do better under pressure or at a steady pace? Habitual or varied? Morning, midday, or evening? Home, outdoors, or a busy coffee shop? Use the answers to design your week's structure.
- Example artist rhythms cited: 2 weeks writing/demoing then 2 weeks on content for the chosen song; or banking 3 months of music then spending 3 months on content for that batch; or splitting the week (Mon/Wed/Fri = music, Tue/Thu/Sat = content).
- Chris's own weekly template, given explicitly: Monday = zero calls, deep creative work only (his freshest day); Tuesday/Wednesday = client calls and agency work; Thursday = pre-production; Friday = productions (shoots, videos, podcast interviews); weekends protected for family and recharging.
- Tooling described: a color-coded Google Calendar paired with Asana for tasks — tasks get pulled into calendar blocks by priority, including non-work items like sleep, snacks, breaks, walks, meditation, school drop-off, and bedtime stories.
- Default meetings to 10 minutes instead of the automatic 30/60-minute default when the content doesn't require more time.
- Maintain layered planning horizons simultaneously (10-year, 5-year, 1-year, 1-month plans) plus a weekly time-blocking session done Sunday evening or Monday morning to set that week's priorities.
- When triaging a week's to-do list, explicitly ask which tasks require you personally versus which can be delegated or dropped.
- Pre-commit protected personal time blocks (even something as small as a specific evening with your partner) so that declining a request has a concrete, legitimate reason behind it.
- Treat the felt sense of encroaching burnout as a trigger to schedule an actual recharge activity immediately, rather than pushing through it.
- Grade your week against a target of about 80% completion of what you time-blocked, not 100%.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Protect one full recurring "zero-call" day each week reserved purely for deep creative work — like Chris's Monday — instead of scattering creative time into whatever gaps are left between calls and errands. The hidden cost most artists underestimate isn't lack of raw hours, it's the constant context-switching between "call brain" and "creative brain"; blocking one whole day (and actually declining calls that day) is a single, concrete change you can make this week that protects far more usable creative output than the same hours spread thin.
