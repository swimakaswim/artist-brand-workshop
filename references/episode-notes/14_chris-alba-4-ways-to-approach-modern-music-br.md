# Ep14 — Chris Alba: 4 Ways To Approach Modern Music Branding
**Length / Source:** Episode 14 | Tue, 19 Nov 2024 | 37m | source: whisper
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Drawing on his path from teenage DJ-club kid to talent buyer who watched artists like Skrillex and Dillon Francis play for $50 a show, Chris Alba argues that the gap between a $200 DJ and a $50,000 headliner is rarely technical skill — it's having an authentic brand and story built from your own genuinely combined tastes, not copied from whatever's already popular.

## Key claims & mental models
- Booking talent for years at Control (Avalon Theater) and its SF sister venue Ruby Sky, Alba watched future headliners (Skrillex, Dillon Francis, Flosstradamus, Valentino Khan) play to near-empty rooms for ~$50/show — direct lived evidence that today's biggest names started from zero draw, not innate specialness.
- The founding "bet" behind Cult Creatives: having been in the studio with artists earning wildly different fees for the same hour-long set, Alba concluded technical skill was "not that big of a difference" pound-for-pound — the real gap was that higher-fee acts had a story, a brand, and a full creative offering (visuals, tour narrative, video narrative) the community could identify with, while lower-fee producers just had "songs or tracks" with no clear identity or position.
- Frames top artists as thinking about "the movie" the audience is watching — the whole experience and its scenes — rather than just the songs, positioning this as the mental shift that separates mid-tier artists from superstars.
- His own exit from touring (stadiums, arenas, festival pyro) wasn't about money or excitement running dry — the constant travel conflicted with wanting to settle down, and he realized the part of his own project he loved most was the creative marketing/branding/strategy layer, not the touring itself.
- Cult Creatives' later pivot into corporate brand collaborations (Alba as creative director bringing "music flavor" into corporate campaigns) grew organically out of relationships built doing music-artist branding, and left the agency more diversified heading into the 2020 pandemic than if it had stayed purely music-focused — though he notes the corporate work felt less personally meaningful than working with artists he'd known "since song release number one."
- Central authenticity advice: look within and at your own roots rather than at what's currently popular — copying an already-successful artist's formula just sends the audience back to the original, since "they've already got that formula" from the artist who owns it; the real question is what you uniquely have.
- Illustrates original-combination-over-competition with a "Korean barbecue burrito" story: a taco truck with a dramatically longer line than its neighbors turned out to be run by a vendor blending his own Korean and Mexican heritage authentically — the lesson is to combine your own distinct tastes into something novel rather than competing head-on in an already crowded lane, where you'd be fighting a lot of existing competition for that exact same territory.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Run a self-audit exercise: list your top 20 favorite singles and top 20 favorite albums and ask why — look for the patterns in your own taste rather than starting from what's trending.
- Build your brand identity from your own authentic, cross-genre tastes (your personal "Korean BBQ burrito") instead of chasing a single already-successful lane owned by someone else.
- Before copying a trend or a successful peer, ask directly: "what do I have that's my unique thing and my unique recipe?" — if the honest answer is nothing beyond what the original already offers, it's not worth pursuing.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Alba's founding insight for Cult Creatives came from literally comparing two artists' fees after having studio time with both: pound-for-pound their technical skill was close, but one earned far more because they showed up with a full story and brand, while the other just had songs. Run that same audit on your own project this week — find an artist charging noticeably more than you for a comparable set, and instead of asking "are they more talented," ask "what's their whole offering: the visuals, the tour narrative, the video narrative, the through-line I could describe in one sentence?" Then run the same audit on yourself. The gap you find is usually brand and story, not musicianship — and unlike musicianship, it's buildable in weeks, not years.
