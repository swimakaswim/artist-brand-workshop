# Flosstradamus: Do It Different. How To Make Your Music Stand Out with Flosstradamus
**Length / Source:** Episode 5 | Tue, 17 Sep 2024 | 73m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Flosstradamus's Curt traces one throughline across turntablism, mashups, trap, NFTs, and AI — that lasting creative growth comes from chasing genuine curiosity and contributing something authentically different to each culture he enters, and that protecting that playful, beginner's-mind relationship to the work (even after fame, pressure, and burnout) is what sustains a long career.

## Key claims & mental models
- A career-long pattern of entering new "cultures" (turntablism, mashups, trap, mopeds, NFTs, AI tools) not to extract from them but to contribute something genuinely unique: "let me give a penny to the culture so I can take a penny from the culture."
- Differentiation functioned as a subconscious instinct more than a stated strategy — illustrated by the host via a Korean-Mexican-burrito-truck analogy: "this is good, and this is good, but you haven't had it like this yet."
- He describes following pure emotional reaction over conscious "self-expression" — never consciously framing work as "expressing myself," just wanting to share things that hit him emotionally and see if others reacted the same way.
- Success and pressure can quietly corrupt the creative process: once something starts working, artists unconsciously shift from "what feels true" to "let me repeat what worked" — identified as the actual cause of both burnout and creative decline, not the workload itself.
- His burnout stemmed from a compounding stack (nonstop touring, a band breakup, a personal breakup, loss of identity — "I lost grip on who I was"); the pandemic forced an externally imposed pause that let him reset.
- His recovery technique is deliberately returning to "the initial seed" / beginner's mind with a project or relationship — treating your relationship to your craft like dating someone again rather than operating from accumulated routine and baggage.
- Cross-pollinating into an unrelated medium (tech/visual work) when one creative outlet feels stale can indirectly restore energy for the original craft — described as "let me go cheat and then come back."
- AI tools (e.g., Suno) are reframed as a new form of "crate digging" — an endless source of raw material trained on all of music, generated repeatedly until one version resonates, then chopped and rebuilt in a DAW, producing the same discovery-feeling as digging for vinyl.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- The literal workflow behind the breakout "Original Don" trap remix: requested the original stems directly from the source artist, stripped the original genre's drums, replaced them with trap drums, added minor builds/switches — a roughly 30-minute edit, deliberately simple rather than over-produced.
- Test genre-blending or mashup ideas live on the dance floor over time before committing them to a produced, released record — let crowd reaction validate the combination first.
- When burned out or creatively stuck, deliberately re-approach the work like its "beginning" — ask "how would I win this over if I didn't already have it," instead of operating on routine.
- If stuck in one medium, temporarily shift creative energy into a completely different one (tech, visual art) as an indirect way to restore energy for the original craft.
- Practical AI-assisted production workflow: prompt full song ideas from an AI tool as raw "samples," regenerate until one resonates, then pull it into a DAW, stem-separate it, and rebuild it with your own drums/production rather than using the raw output as-is.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Most creative advice says "find what's working and do more of it" — this guest's hard-earned lesson is closer to the opposite: the unconscious shift from making what feels true to repeating what already worked is what quietly causes both burnout and creative decline. The concrete antidote he uses is a repeatable ritual, not a one-time fix — deliberately re-approach your current project (or your relationship to your craft generally) like the early "dating" stage: ask what you'd try if you still had to win it over, rather than defaulting to what you know already works.
