# 20 Years of Music Industry Knowledge in 30 mins
**Length / Source:** Tue, 28 Jan 2025 | 29m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Across 20 years working alongside top touring music artists, the traits that separate artists who reach and stay at the top from mid-tier acts are mostly mindset and discipline — making art for yourself, having a real mission, treating marketing with as much craft as the music, and sustaining obsessive growth — more than raw talent.

## Key claims & mental models
- Lesson 1 — Make it for you first, keep it fresh: citing Rick Rubin's "audience last" philosophy, the top producers he's observed make choices for their own taste, not the audience's; he contrasts "system hackers" (gaming follower/streaming metrics) against craft-obsessed artists, and says the craft-obsessed type consistently ends up bigger. Staying fresh requires deliberately re-challenging yourself (new collaborators, travel, leaving your comfort zone) — letting the audience dictate direction eventually goes stale because the artist stops being excited first.
- Lesson 2 — Have a mission: a mission (vs. a hobby) produces a visibly different energy and urgency — illustrated by an artist performing through a 103°F fever, throwing up in a bucket beside the DJ booth mid-set, with the audience never noticing — and purpose is named as the actual source of artists' joy and fulfillment, not just an output driver.
- Lesson 3 — "How many people can you bring to the party?": the business side of music is measured purely by tickets/merch/streams moved — not tenure, likability, or work ethic — and that's presented as a fair, simple fact of how the business functions rather than something to resent.
- Lesson 4 — Perform like you're already a headliner: full commitment even to a near-empty room signals a self-belief that becomes self-fulfilling — a then-unknown Lady Gaga performing all-out (carried onstage by four men, full theatrical energy) to roughly 50 people at a small club, and the electro-house duo Dada Life going full energy (champagne, bananas, choreography) to a half-cleared room at 3am at the Avalon — both cases he predicted, correctly, would become huge. Caveat: this isn't license to overplay an opening slot — reading the room and serving your slot appropriately is called "a lost art" in itself.
- Lesson 5 — Treat branding/advertising as seriously as the music: top artist teams plan marketing ~24 months out around clear tentpole moments; mid-tier teams plan only 4-6 months and lean mainly on release schedules; newer artists often have no calendar at all and post reactively. Also: promotional channels keep changing (newspaper ads → street teams → email/text → digital ad buys) and artists need to keep adopting new channels rather than being sentimental about old ones.
- Lesson 6 — Your point of view/taste is everything: top artists draw inspiration from their own life and experience, not from watching peers in their genre; mid-tier artists mimic other artists and "chase the dragon" from trend to trend, which caps them at "the middle" rather than the top.
- Lessons 7-9 (financial/legal discipline): save money and/or invest in appreciating assets — multifamily real estate specifically named as common among artists he knows — because every career has dips; don't quit your day job purely to buy time before music income is real, since injecting "survival" stakes into the studio degrades decision-making (Arnold Schwarzenegger's bricklaying-then-real-estate-investing path cited as a model for choosing roles/work strategically rather than out of financial desperation); always own your masters, flagged as the single most common regret he hears from artists.
- Lesson 10 — Health is paramount: nearly all the top touring artists he knows practice cold plunging and (per his observation) roughly 95% don't drink heavily or use drugs, framed not as anti-rockstar but as necessary to sustain sharpness across packed touring/business schedules.
- Lesson 11 — Obsession beats talent: named as the single unifying trait across every top artist he's worked with — constant, voracious pursuit of improvement, paired with obsessive team-building (hiring for caliber/pedigree over cost, on the logic that "you're the sum of the five people you spend the most time with").
- Lesson 12 — Make it remarkable: when clients come to him with a "broken brand" (nothing seems to land), the root cause is almost never the music itself — it's that nothing about the surrounding content/story gives anyone a reason to tell someone else about it; the fix is running ideas through the filter "is this remarkable, would someone actually remark about it."

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Apply Rick Rubin's "audience last" approach in the studio — make the call based on your own taste first.
- Deliberately schedule "freshness" inputs on a recurring basis: travel, new collaborators, time outside your usual scene.
- Build out a marketing/content calendar with real lead time around named tentpole moments, working toward planning much further ahead than feels natural (large artists plan ~24 months out, as a benchmark to grow into).
- Direct a portion of music income toward appreciating assets by default — multifamily real estate given as the specific, repeated example among artists he knows.
- Hold off leaving your day job until music is genuinely your sustaining income, not just to free up more working hours.
- Treat owning your masters as non-negotiable.
- Hire collaborators/team based on caliber and fit, not lowest cost, when filling skill gaps.
- Before shipping any release or content idea, run it through the remarkable filter: would this actually make someone text a friend "have you seen this yet?" — if not, the fix is the story/marketing, not (necessarily) the music.

## Highest-leverage insight
> When something you've made isn't landing, his default diagnosis (built from actually being hired to fix "broken" artist brands) is almost never "the music isn't good enough" — it's that nothing in the surrounding story or content gives anyone a reason to tell someone else about it. Before your next release or post, explicitly ask "would this make someone text a friend 'have you seen this yet?'" If the honest answer is no, treat it as a fixable storytelling/marketing gap rather than a referendum on your talent — this reframes the anxiety spiral of "my content isn't performing" into a specific, actionable question you can test this week.
