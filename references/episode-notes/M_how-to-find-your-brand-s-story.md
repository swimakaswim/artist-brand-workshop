# How to Find Your Brand's Story
**Length / Source:** source: youtube | https://youtu.be/1Cs-ZRpwa3Y
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Chris Alba argues that an artist brand needs a deliberately chosen "plot" — one of Christopher Booker's seven basic story structures — layered on top of its brand personality, because plot, not personality or aesthetics alone, is the specific mechanism that makes an audience psychologically bond to and keep following an artist's ongoing "show."

## Key claims & mental models
- An artist brand functions like an ongoing movie the audience is watching: personality archetype answers "who is this character," plot answers "what is the story" — both are required, and plot is the less-obvious, more overlooked half.
- Five psychological reasons plot specifically drives audience bonding: pattern-seeking (brains are wired to track beginning/middle/end arcs), tension/curiosity loops (unresolved stakes compel people to keep watching), emotional engagement (investment in a character's struggle and growth), comfort (a structured story lowers cognitive load versus "random," which loses attention), and identification/reflection (universal plot themes let a viewer project themselves into the artist's story).
- Seven basic plots (from Christopher Booker's book The Seven Basic Plots, claimed as exhaustive across mythology, Shakespeare, and modern film/TV): Overcoming the Monster, Rags to Riches, The Quest, Voyage and Return, Comedy, Tragedy, Rebirth.
- Plots can be mixed the same way personality archetypes can, but one plot needs to clearly "lead" so the audience isn't confused about what story they're actually watching.
- Because new followers can discover an artist at any random point in the story, content needs to periodically re-establish the core stakes (the "monster," the goal) rather than assuming every viewer already has the full backstory.
- Once a plot is chosen, the main discipline is not deviating from the central challenge/story — consistency of narrative, not just visual consistency, is what makes the plot legible over time.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Pick one of the seven plots (or a lead plot plus a secondary blend) that actually matches your real story, then explicitly define its structural beats: the calling/threat, the setback, the confrontation, the low point, and eventually the resolution.
- Use interviews and long-form content specifically to plant origin-story and plot details in the audience's mind — cited as how Lady Gaga's early "too weird, not marketable" industry-rejection story got established as her plot's setup.
- Worked reference examples by plot, usable as swipe material: Overcoming the Monster — Taylor Swift (media/fame as the monster) and Lady Gaga (industry rejection as the monster); Rags to Riches — Post Malone (kept his scrappy bedroom-producer origin visible even after mainstream success) and Eminem (Detroit origin, battling fame/drugs/inner demons), plus Gary Vee as an example of naming the "mountain" explicitly (his stated goal of buying the New York Jets) so the audience knows exactly what the endgame is; The Quest — Donald Glover/Childish Gambino (an explicit multi-medium "I want to do what I want to do" quote used as plot-setup) and Porter Robinson (an inward quest through depression and creative rediscovery, framed as a quest that can be open-ended and never fully resolved); Voyage and Return — Gorillaz and Diplo; Comedy — Dillon Francis, Tenacious D, Lonely Island; Tragedy — Linkin Park, The Weeknd, and painter Edvard Munch; Rebirth — Britney Spears (post-conservatorship) and Demi Lovato (addiction-to-sobriety narrative).
- Mixed-plot calibration examples given: The Weeknd = Rags to Riches + Tragedy; Illenium = Rebirth + Tragedy (his phoenix-rising logo; early work centered on heartbreak, relapse, and emotional collapse); Skrillex = Quest + Overcoming the Monster, with the monster named specifically as the music industry, genre stigma, and gatekeeping — literalized in his album title Quest for Fire.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Pick one of the seven plots as your project's primary structure this week, and write one paragraph naming your specific "monster" or "mountain" as concretely as Gary Vee names buying the NFL's Jets — not "the industry" in the abstract, but the specific obstacle, stigma, or climb you're actually fighting. Then check your next 3-5 pieces of content against it: each one should reference some part of that named struggle (the calling, a setback, or progress on the climb) instead of being a disconnected post, because a new follower could be discovering you at any random point in the story and needs the stakes re-established, not assumed.
