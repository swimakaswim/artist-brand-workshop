# A Single Method Top Artists Use To Make Millions
**Length / Source:** Episode 31 | Wed, 21 May 2025 | 24m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Elite DJs don't succeed on one gift or lucky break — they make one overarching choice, extreme and sustained commitment, and apply it across every key facet of "the game" (music, story/brand, execution, awareness, and quality control), not just studio time.

## Key claims & mental models
- Central model borrowed from Kobe Bryant's well-known work-ethic account: most people who reach a high level (the NBA, or a viable music career) stop pushing once the initial dream ("making it") is achieved; the ones who become truly elite keep asking "how do I become the best," which requires a different, longer-horizon kind of commitment.
- Crucially, Kobe's extra hours weren't just more of the same drill — he rounded out all facets of his game (offense, defense, ball movement, rebounding, mindset/leadership). The analogous mistake for artists is assuming more studio time alone is the answer.
- Maps basketball's multi-skill model onto five proposed facets of "the music artist game": (1) the music/production itself, (2) story, branding, and positioning — treated as its own craft requiring script-writing-level skill, not an afterthought, (3) "producing" in the broader sense — executing and bringing all content to life, distinct from music production, (4) awareness/advertising — understanding platforms and audience psychology well enough that people actually find and value the work, (5) quality control — the discipline of only releasing work that's genuinely at your standard.
- Reframes advertising as something creatives instinctively resist (most artists want to "just make the art"), but argues embracing it — rather than treating it as beneath the craft — is what actually gets great art in front of people: "the business part of it goes hand in hand with the art."
- Quality control has two components: (a) deep, deliberate study of great work specifically to be able to articulate *why* it's great, not just that you like it, and (b) personal/trauma work (therapy, journaling, coaching), because unresolved personal issues distort self-assessment — either holding work back too long ("it's not there yet" when it's been ready for weeks) or rushing out subpar work out of desperation for income/relevance.
- Frames the whole pursuit as "a one-on-one game, you versus you" — the top artists he's worked with are ultimately driven by self-improvement against their own past output, not by beating other artists or even primarily by pleasing fans.
- Uses a one-focus-per-year model for skill-building rather than trying to improve at everything simultaneously (his own producer origin story: year one dedicated solely to synthesis/sound design, year two solely to drums).

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Structure self-teaching by giving each defined period (a year, a quarter) a single narrow focus rather than spreading attention across every skill at once.
- For story/brand skill-building: take relevant craft courses (e.g. comedy writing, if your brand is comedic), actively analyze films/shows for what specifically works rather than just watching passively, and read books on branding, story, and screenwriting.
- Learn adjacent craft skills directly when they serve the brand — cites Don Diablo teaching himself graphic design because his brand needed to be visually driven and he wanted to communicate his own ideas directly.
- When hiring a designer or creative director, explicitly ask them to teach you as you collaborate rather than only accepting a finished deliverable.
- For awareness/advertising skill-building: watch tutorials on advertising and marketing trends, read books or take classes on the psychology of advertising and consumer behavior, and work with vetted mentors from the advertising world.
- For quality control: deliberately study work you consider great and force yourself to articulate specifically why it moves you; pursue therapy, meditation, journaling, or coaching to reduce the personal bias that distorts judgment of your own work.
- Modeled example of extreme investment: early in his career, Skrillex reportedly spent his own money traveling to session with established artists (e.g. Noisia) instead of taking a vacation, and his music noticeably leveled up afterward.
- Cites Jerry Seinfeld reportedly writing every single day for over 40 years as a model of the same one-choice, sustained-commitment habit applied to comedy.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Instead of treating "get better" as one vague, all-at-once goal, identify which single facet of the game — music, story/brand, execution, awareness/advertising, or quality control — is currently your weakest link, and give it one defined, exclusive focus period (a month, a quarter, a year), the way the host dedicated year one of learning production purely to sound design and year two purely to drums. Most artists spread thin effort across everything simultaneously and plateau on all fronts at once; deliberately narrowing to one underdeveloped facet for a fixed stretch is the actual mechanism behind the "extreme commitment" this episode credits for turning working DJs into headliners.
