# How to Improve Your Music Brand by STEALING From Hollywood - For Music Artists & Musicians
**Length / Source:** Episode 29 | Tue, 06 May 2025 | 26m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Independent artists should borrow Hollywood's screenwriting toolkit — character, setting, conflict, logline, visual identity, and transformation arc — to build a deliberate, ongoing "story system," because attention and connection, not music quality alone, are now the primary bottleneck to growth.

## Key claims & mental models
- Reframes the core problem: the biggest obstacle isn't the music itself, it's attention and awareness — great songs that nobody understands or connects with don't move the needle.
- Key structural difference from film: a music audience experiences the "story" in disconnected fragments over time (posts, clips, moments) rather than start-to-finish in one sitting, so the story system has to hold together across scattered touchpoints, not just within one piece of content.
- Step 1, Identity/casting: great writers start with character psychology before plot — what the character wants, fears, and what's "broken" inside them (Joker = chaos vs. order; Walter White = wants respect). For an artist-as-brand, this means consciously deciding what parts of yourself to reveal vs. withhold — organized into archetype, strengths, flaws, beliefs, and contradictions.
- Step 2, Setting: environment is itself a storytelling device that should mirror the character's identity or emotional state (Blade Runner 2049's isolation, Mad Max's chaos, La La Land's LA-as-dream-vs-reality vs. Heat's cold, detached LA — same city, opposite narrative texture). A low-budget counter-example (a New Orleans-based artist filming on buses, in vintage stores, on street corners) is used to show setting-driven storytelling doesn't require a big budget, just intentional use of what's already around you.
- Step 3, Conflict: "a character wants something but something is in the way" drives every scene — for artists, define tension across internal, external, industry, and personal buckets (Eminem's genius vs. self-destruction; Kanye's visionary confidence vs. ego/self-sabotage; Billie Eilish's vulnerability vs. fear of fame; Taylor Swift's narrative mastery vs. need for control). Some artists are their own antagonist; others battle external foes like media and critics.
- Step 4, Logline: one sentence should capture the whole story, using the formula "a [archetype] artist who [does X] is trying to [Y] but struggles with [Z]," then expanded into a short narrative-style bio (worked examples for Dom Dolla and Leon Bridges). Uniqueness should come from blending multiple reference points into a new combination rather than copying one artist directly — "predictable equals boring."
- Step 5, Visual identity: cinematography-style choices (color, lighting, styling) should reinforce the story, not just be picked because they're "cool" (Tyler the Creator's era-based color/art-direction shifts, Rezz's consistent visual codes, Ice Spice's DIY 2000s-nostalgia look).
- Step 6, Transformation/arc: real stories require change — define both short arcs (per era/release) and a long arc (across a career), since audiences stay engaged by watching where the story goes next (Gwen Stefani's rebel-to-mature arc, Rihanna's princess-to-queen arc).
- Fans use an artist's story to define their own identity (wearing a shirt signals self-identity, not just fandom) — the clearer and stronger the story, the deeper that identification and the more organic sharing it generates.
- All six elements should function as an ongoing "story system" — a standing filter for every future content and campaign decision, not a one-time branding exercise.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Run a personal "idea dump" journaling session (alone or with a friend): who is this character, what do they want, what are they afraid of, what's broken inside them — then organize the answers into archetype / strengths / flaws / beliefs / contradictions.
- Explicitly name your story's recurring setting(s) and their tone, using resources you actually have access to (a specific neighborhood, a type of local venue) rather than assuming you need a Rufus Du Sol- or Bad Bunny-scale budget.
- Write your ongoing tension across four buckets: internal, external, industry, personal.
- Write a one-sentence logline using the fill-in-the-blank formula ("A [archetype] kind of artist who [X] is trying to [Y] but struggles with [Z]"), then expand it into a short story-style bio.
- Define visual identity choices (colors, fonts, photo/video style, textures) deliberately so they reinforce the story, rather than defaulting to "does this look cool."
- Map your arc explicitly: where you are now, where you're going, and what the next chapter's change will be — treat each release era like a TV season with its own mini arc feeding a longer career-length arc.
- The host invited viewers to post their own one-line or one-paragraph elevator pitch in the comments for direct feedback — a concrete prompt to actually write the exercise down rather than just think about it.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Force your artist story into one sentence using the formula "A [archetype] kind of artist who [does X] is trying to [Y] but struggles with [Z]." If you can't fill that in cleanly, your content is very likely disconnected across posts because you're improvising a new mini-story each time instead of running everything through one filter. Write that sentence this week, then check your last 10 posts against it — the ones that reinforce no part of the sentence are exactly the "posting to post" content this episode says audiences scroll past unnoticed.
