# What the F*ck to Actually Post for Content as a Music Artist
**Length / Source:** Episode 34 | Wed, 17 Sep 2025 | 26m | source: youtube
**Thesis (1 sentence):** "What should I post?" is only answerable by zooming from your overarching brand story down through era, campaign, and content buckets to individual formats and platforms — and once that funnel exists, content ideas become mechanical to generate and just need to be planned on a calendar and executed.

## Key claims & mental models
- Asking "is this post good or bad" without context is unanswerable — a post only makes sense in light of the full brand story ("the movie"), so story must be defined before any content judgment or creation happens.
- Brand/story is estimated at roughly 70-80% of a music project's success; the music is a part of it the way a soundtrack is part of a movie, not the whole picture.
- Artists move through distinct eras/periods without abandoning their core story — cites Kanye West's College Dropout/808s/Pablo eras, Picasso's Blue Period, the Beatles' psychedelic Revolver/Sgt. Pepper's period, and Virgil Abloh's "figures of speech" era as a cultural-thinker phase. Each era defines a content "sandbox."
- A campaign is a smaller, targeted story (tied to a release, tour, or show) that ties into the larger brand movie — illustrated with Tyler the Creator's "Call Me If You Get Lost" rollout (mystery billboards with a phone number, a cinematic teaser, passport/luxury-travel visual world) and the horror film "Smile"'s creepy-smile stunt marketing as a low-budget, high-creativity campaign example.
- Content buckets are mid-level themed idea pools derived from brand archetype + current campaign concept (worked examples: an "Explorer" archetype album shot around global travel/collaborators yields buckets like travel, adventure stories, world cultures; a "Rebel" archetype DIY mixtape yields buckets like rags-to-riches moments, mock-surveillance-footage aesthetics, high-energy performance clips).
- The social algorithms shifted from follower-count-based reach to individual-post-value-based reach — a small account's post can now out-reach a large account's if it's watched, liked, shared, and commented on enough; the "playing field" is more even than before.
- Distinguish three content types most larger artists mix: campaign-tied content, ongoing "content properties" (recurring formats tied to the whole brand), and one-off posts — don't rely on only one type.
- A "format" is a repeatable structure (man-on-the-street, day-in-the-life vlog, hot takes, blind-listen reactions); most good short-form content follows hook → lean-in → story → punchline/payoff.
- Format and platform choice should be filtered through brand archetype and real personality — e.g., an introverted "creator" archetype shouldn't force on-camera skits, but could do cinematic voiceover/B-roll with music as the score instead.
- "Don't make broccoli" — don't just re-chop one piece of content into smaller versions for every platform; make content native to each platform's specific audience and style.
- Go deep on a few platforms rather than wide across all of them, unless already a mainstage headliner — pick where your specific audience actually spends time.
- Planning horizon correlates with career stage: bigger artists plan 4-6 months ahead, mid-tier artists 1-2 months, newer artists often have no plan at all.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Build a content calendar; the host offers a free template (comment "cult calendar" on the original video).
- Monthly content-mapping process: (1) list tentpole items for the month — releases, show announces, shows already played — and block placeholders for each (a single release might need ~10 posts by itself), (2) fill remaining calendar days using ideas pulled from your content-bucket list, (3) balance storytelling/connection posts against awareness/marketing posts rather than over-indexing on either.
- Quantity vs. quality posting volume is a personal choice, not right or wrong — cites Alex Hormozi's reported ~350 pieces of content per week across platforms as one end of the spectrum, versus artists who post only a handful of times a year but generate millions of views per post as the other end.
- Format-selection checklist: does it tie to the current campaign? Is it aligned with your personality/story? Would it resonate with your specific community? Is it actually achievable with your current resources?
- Platform-selection checklist: what platforms do you want to build community on, are they aligned with your brand and audience, and can you execute there consistently?
- Content-analysis checklist (post-publish): does this feel aligned to me and my story, am I chasing performance or purpose, am I telling my story or just chasing views, and is this the best I could make with the resources I have?
- Case studies of "content properties": a client (Wookie) built a recurring two-character reaction-video format around unreleasable bootleg remixes ("Beats I Can't Release") that went viral repeatedly, was reused roughly 200 times, and changed his career trajectory — some of the sampled originals were later officially cleared because rights holders saw the remix's reception. Steve Aoki's mid-air jump photos, taken in different locations worldwide and numbered sequentially, became a long-running, ownable content property.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Build your content-bucket list this week — 5 to 6 recurring themes derived from your brand archetype and current campaign — then use only that list to fill in next month's calendar before you post anything else. The episode frames the artists who stall as the ones improvising post ideas from scratch every day (the "I haven't posted in a while, I guess I should post something" trap); the ones who grow have pre-generated a bucket of on-brand ideas and are just pulling from it on a schedule. That single shift — from daily invention to monthly extraction — is what actually separates consistent output from sporadic output.
