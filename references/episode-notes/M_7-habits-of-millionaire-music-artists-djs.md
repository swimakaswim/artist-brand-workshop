# 7 Habits of Millionaire Music Artists & DJs That You Can STEAL
**Length / Source:** source: youtube | https://youtu.be/c_9ef-SGWsM
**Thesis (1 sentence):** Chris Alba distills 20 years of working alongside major dance-music artists — Skrillex, Chris Lake, James Hype, Steve Aoki, Alice in Wonderland, A-Trak — into seven concrete habits (authenticity, intentionality, athlete-level content discipline, treating brand as "the movie" around the music, performing as an entertainer, personally leading your own project, and continuously feeding your creative input), arguing that what separates million-dollar artists from everyone else is sustained daily habit, not talent or luck.

## Key claims & mental models
- Chasing trends is self-defeating by definition ("every trend you chase is already on its way out") — authenticity is framed as a durable structural advantage, not just a values statement, because audiences can perceptibly tell true from performed, illustrated by Skrillex making a genre-blending track "for himself first," which initially confused dancefloors before "changing the game."
- Being yourself and knowing your audience aren't in tension — trusting your own uniqueness, rather than leading with audience-first imitation, is framed as what makes work resonate deeper.
- Intentionality protects against becoming purely reactive; the Chris Lake case (deliberately abandoning a working big-room sound for a slower-building underground identity in 2014, without changing his artist name) shows that aligning sound with a genuinely chosen identity — even at short-term cost — reads to audiences as clarity and trust, versus chasing whatever's exciting that week.
- Content is reframed from "marketing task" to trained discipline, explicitly modeled on athletic practice — consistency, not perfection, is what "builds rhythm" that "creates breakthroughs," and even top-tier content output (James Hype, nicknamed "La Machina" by fans) was built "over years of tiny efforts," not born.
- Brand is defined as "the movie" running alongside the music-as-"soundtrack," argued to be a distinct, comparably important craft — proven with a paired fee comparison where a spectacle-driving act (Dot Life) was booked at roughly 20x the fee of a technically comparable studio act with no brand, story, or in-show experience beyond the hits.
- Performance and entertainment value are treated as separable from musicianship — a great track earns one listen, but a memorable performance or spectacle (Steve Aoki's stage-diving, raft-riding, cake tosses) earns return visits, word-of-mouth, and sustained conversation, independent of whether the spectacle itself is universally liked.
- Leadership is reframed away from control or doing-everything toward owning outcomes, priorities, and vision-holding — most artist stalling is attributed to a "leadership gap" (lack of clarity/direction) rather than a talent gap, even for artists who already reached a mid-level plateau.
- Creativity is modeled as a muscle requiring active, continuous outside-genre input ("tension, fuel, and rest") rather than a well you passively draw from — the underlying craft of art is reframed as referencing and remixing an accumulated personal "reservoir" of cross-domain influences, not generating from nothing.
- Overarching frame: none of the seven habits are one-time actions — every example given is a multi-year pattern, and the closing argument is explicit that most artists won't sustain this level of seriousness, the way most aspiring pro athletes never reach the highest tier.

## Concrete tactics (do-this)
- Filter every new piece of work through "is this me? Does this represent what I care about?" — built from a personal inventory of what you've loved across your past and present.
- Journal explicitly on: what kind of career do I want, what rooms do I want to play, what do I want my music to represent in 5-10 years — then commit and reverse-engineer decisions backward from that target ("start with the end in mind").
- Build one small, sustainable content habit first, small enough to hold for 6 months, then stack a second only once the first is truly locked in — avoid adopting many habits simultaneously ("discipline drunk").
- Run any bigger content, press, or show moment through four questions before executing: What makes this entertaining? What makes this memorable? Why would people talk about it? Would it catch the interest of someone who has no idea who you are?
- Build literal structure around your own leadership: set timelines, monthly goals, and track content/outreach/creative-growth progress; pursue therapy specifically as a tool for building the internal clarity and confidence that makes decisive leadership easier.
- Deliberately schedule exposure outside your own genre or scene — unfamiliar-genre shows, international film, old album cover design, conversations with fashion designers, photographers, or filmmakers about their process — and treat it as active creative input, not a detour from "real" work.
- Push into creative discomfort on purpose: writing sessions or collaborations you're not fully comfortable with, sharing ideas with non-musicians, to let outside perspective challenge default instincts.

## Highest-leverage insight
> Of the seven, the most immediately actionable is the content-discipline habit: instead of trying to match a "content machine" artist's output volume, pick one piece of content you can commit to producing consistently for six months — something small enough that skipping it isn't an option, as low-friction as Alba's own example of a daily walk to the mailbox — and don't add a second content habit until the first is genuinely automatic. The James Hype case study is explicit that his nickname "the machine" was earned over years of small, unglamorous reps, not innate output — most artists instead try to launch a full content system at once, burn out in weeks, and conclude they're "just not a content person." Starting absurdly small and single-threaded is the actual mechanism, not a lesser version of the goal.
